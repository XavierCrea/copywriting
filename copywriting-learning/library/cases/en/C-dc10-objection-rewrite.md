# DC-10: answer the actual concern before presenting technical reassurance

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-dc10-objection-rewrite.md) · Critique and hypothetical rewrite

- **Context:** a McDonnell Douglas public-trust advertisement; the author's proposed rewrite has no confirmed publication record.
- **Reader:** people who know about a crash and question aircraft safety.
- **Task:** address the concern and rebuild company credibility.
- **Medium:** excerpts from the original print ad, criticism, and a hypothetical rewrite.

## What the material shows

- Former astronaut and divisional vice president Peter Conrad signs the original ad. Manufacturing hours, wind-tunnel and fatigue testing, and operating scale support its standards; an address offers further information.
- The author criticizes it for never directly naming the crash and public concern that prompted the advertising, instead proceeding to credentials and tests.
- On pp. 219–220, the author explicitly proposes what he would write: acknowledge the accident and deaths, discuss causes and corrections, address other accidents and inspections, then reconnect to the original professional testimony.

## How the writing works

The problem is a mismatch between evidence and question. The original has substantial technical material, but readers want to know what the accident means and what has changed. General standards answer a different question and may read as evasion when the negative event is already known. A direct acknowledgment gives the later evidence the context it needs.

The reusable structure is concern, verified facts, corrective action, and evidence of effectiveness, reinforced by credentials and broader capability. Retaining useful original passages also shows that a rewrite need not discard everything: it may need a missing premise. The causes, corrections, and inspection frequency in the proposed text are hypothetical copy, not independently established company actions. The observable result is a structural contrast between texts; improved public sentiment remains the author's prediction, with no audience test.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Name the issue the reader already knows but the draft avoids.
2. Verify the event, impact, actions taken, and available evidence; keep unknowns explicit.
3. Address the chief objection first, then explain the response and how its effectiveness is checked.
4. Retain relevant technical support and offer access to further material.

## Draft checks

- Does the opening answer the concern or advertise a corporate strength?
- Is every cause, correction, and safety statement supported?
- Is authority being used instead of an answer?

## Suitable tasks

- A clearly established negative perception requires an explanation, correction of misunderstanding, or account of remedial action.

## Transfer limits

- Accident causes and engineering or historical details here remain the book's account, not this project's independent findings.
- Changing inspections from 100 to 50 hours belongs to the hypothetical rewrite, not verified McDonnell Douglas practice or reusable technical advice.

## Reported results and evidence

The author predicts a favorable impression. No actual placement, perception change, or sales result for the rewrite is documented.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original structural exercise:** “[Issue] affected [scope]. We have [verified action]. Here is how [verification] checks the result.”

Concrete nouns establish scope; active verbs make responsibility visible. Keep the action and its evidence adjacent. Complete the brackets only from confirmed facts; fluent reassurance cannot establish that a remedy occurred.
