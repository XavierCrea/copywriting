# Scale editing: bring the rewarding moment forward, then remove idle words

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-scale-editing-exercise.md) · Writing exercise

- **Context:** a bathroom scale.
- **Reader:** someone trying to lose weight and hoping to see progress.
- **Task:** preserve recognition and reward while conveying the product's value sooner.
- **Medium:** draft and revised first two paragraphs of an ad, with a classroom exercise.

## What the material shows

- After recognizing that losing weight is difficult, the draft explains that a program needs a scale, compares it to a report card, and only then presents the pleasure of seeing a welcome number.
- The revision moves that pleasure immediately after the shared difficulty, followed by the feedback/report-card explanation. It removes detours such as telling experienced readers that they already know and combines repeated definitions.
- The 66-to-43 count on pp. 107–108 covers English words in the first two paragraphs: approximately 34.8% fewer. The almost-40% statement on p. 108 concerns the second paragraph. A footnote identifies English word counts; p. 109 separately lists trimming, rhythm, combining sentences, and reordering.

## How the writing works

The important choice is what readers should feel before deciding which words remain. Both openings acknowledge difficulty. The draft then interrupts that recognition with explanation; the revision immediately evokes the pleasure of seeing progress, gives the product an anticipated role, and uses the report-card analogy to explain it. Moving experience before explanation shortens the path from recognition to desire.

Every deletion has a job: empty lead-ins delay facts, repeated subjects and definitions consume attention, and combined sentences tighten relationships. Yet shared difficulty, a welcome number, and feedback each do different work and should not all disappear in pursuit of brevity. The author notes that students lacked the complete ad and therefore its full sales context. The shortest local passage is not necessarily the best whole ad. Preserve meaning and feeling while reducing unnecessary reading.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Label sentences as recognition, experience, explanation, evidence, or action; identify indispensable jobs.
2. Try moving the most palpable use moment before the abstract explanation.
3. Remove empty lead-ins, combine duplicated definitions, and vary sentence length.
4. Read both versions aloud against the complete objective, checking information, feeling, and transitions.

## Draft checks

- Was repetition cut, or a necessary fact?
- Does the reader encounter a relevant experience earlier?
- Did shortening lose rhythm, conditions, or context?

## Suitable tasks

- Substantive existing copy whose opening or length needs improvement.

## Transfer limits

- The 66-to-43 calculation and almost-40% claim have different denominators; Chinese character counts cannot verify English word counts.
- Pleasure at weight-loss progress is the expression's context, not evidence that a scale causes weight loss.

## Reported results and evidence

The two versions and English counts can be compared directly. No sales or conversion change caused by the edit is reported.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original exercise with a fictional camera:** “The camera provides a convenient way to capture moments quickly” → “Your child blows out the candles. You catch the smile.”

The revision starts with a moment instead of an abstract product description. Short clauses control pace. It is a creative hypothesis, not a camera performance claim; the scenario and ability to capture it must fit the brief. An edit should preserve required facts, not merely swap explanation for emotion.
