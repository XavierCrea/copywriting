# Keep dialogue attribution in the background | Writing better dialogue

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-vgc-writing-better-dialogue.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

## Source observations
The author diagnoses distracting, frequent speaker tags rather than poor dialogue. He recommends omitting tags when speakers are clear, otherwise favoring said or asked without adverbs. Tags can also divide long speech and control rhythm.

## Interpretation and application
Let readers attend to what people say instead of repeated instructions about emotion. Omission works only while speakers remain identifiable. Use when attribution or emotional adverbs repeatedly interrupt a scene.

1. Read aloud and mark every speaker tag.
2. Remove safely inferable tags and simplify the rest.
3. Reread for speaker identity, pauses, and emotional clarity.

Check whether removing a tag causes confusion and whether speech or verified action carries the feeling.

## Limits and results
This is a style preference, not a universal rule of English grammar. Multiple speakers or long gaps can require identification. The teaching conversation supplies no complete student manuscript, measured revision outcome, or marketing result.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original fictional dialogue:**

> “You kept the receipt?”
> “I kept every receipt.”
> She opened the drawer. “Since the first day.”

“Every” expands the significance of the singular “receipt.” The drawer action attributes the next line and gives the claim a physical setting. The final fragment extends its duration. In a longer scene, add a plain “she said” where necessary; clarity takes priority over removing every tag.
