# Avis: turn a competitive position into a service reason readers can inspect

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-avis-service-positioning.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** a challenger must explain why buyers should choose it over the leader.
- **Reader:** renters seeking a reliable car, less hassle, and shorter waits.
- **Task:** explain a service incentive through competitive position, supported by checkable details.

## What the material shows

- Two ads on PDF p. 17 connect second place with greater effort and use large/small fish imagery. Ogilvy credits DDB; he had declined the business because of a client conflict. This is not his own creative work.
- One execution uses the small fish's need to keep swimming, then lists clean ashtrays, filled tanks, battery and wiper checks, and attention at the counter.
- The other asks why to choose the second-place company. Dirty ashtrays, partly filled tanks, faulty wipers, unwashed cars, worn tires, seats, heaters, and defrosters become errors it cannot afford if it needs every customer.
- It wants travelers to leave smiling, offers to tell them where to find a hot sandwich in Duluth, and closes with an invitation and a shorter-queue benefit.
- The author admires the positioning and believes it pressures Hertz. These pages give no Avis sales, profit, share, or controlled-test figures.

## How the writing works

Second place can suggest inferiority. Naming it supplies a motive: a company that needs each customer has a reason not to neglect service. That explains effort rather than simply declaring superiority. The fish make the pressure quickly legible.

Service details complete the argument. Ashtrays, tanks, wipers, and defrosters are things travelers immediately experience. They turn brand character into an inspectable handover checklist. The risk of failures connects the company's incentives to the customer's experience, though a plausible motive is still not delivery evidence.

The sandwich detail extends care from the car to a person in an unfamiliar city. Shorter queues translate smaller scale into immediate convenience. The progression is competitive position, incentive, delivery standards, human care, and choice. Find a real operating condition that may benefit customers and demonstrate it through actual behavior; second place is this case's material, not a reusable headline formula.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Identify why buyers choose the leader and what they question about this brand.
2. Verify whether the operating situation creates a useful incentive; do not invent rankings.
3. Explain the incentive in a concise causal sentence.
4. Select meaningful customer-journey actions and turn them into delivery standards.
5. Add a real, human-scale care detail.
6. Offer a concrete convenience and next step; assess delivery and market response separately.

## Draft checks

- Is the position true and worth foregrounding?
- Does the motive benefit the buyer rather than ask for sympathy?
- Are standards deliverable and inspectable?
- Do details cover important experiences rather than convenient trivia?
- Is there a reason to choose now?
- Does the argument assume unsupported competitor failures?

## Suitable tasks

- A real competitive or operating circumstance explains investment in service.
- Buyers value small delivery frictions and the company can consistently address them.

## Transfer limits

- Historical rankings, cars, service, and queues are not current facts.
- Incentive logic does not replace performance evidence.
- Admitting disadvantage is useful only when that fact can support a customer benefit.

## Reported results and evidence

The ads supply positioning and service claims; Ogilvy supplies praise for DDB. No independently checkable Avis sales, profit, or comparison data appear. Nearby Volkswagen sales figures belong to another case.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original exercise for a fictional service with a real assigned contact:** “One contact. From the first question to the final delivery.”

The repeated journey boundaries make continuity concrete. The claim requires that the same contact actually covers that scope. This borrows the move from an operating practice to a buyer convenience, not Avis's rank or slogan.
