# APF clock calculator: connect a new feature to the buyer’s work before selling it

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-sugarman-apf-clock-calculator.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** a 1973 APF desktop calculator adding a clock display while idle.
- **Reader:** buyers using calculators as business tools in that period.
- **Task:** assess whether the feature and premium create a reason to buy.
- **Medium:** a *Wall Street Journal* test ad, described retrospectively.

## What the material shows

- Page 156 describes a $69.95 calculator and a proposed $99.95 version displaying time while idle. The manufacturer regards it as revolutionary.
- Sugarman believes buyers switch a calculator on when needed and off otherwise; an always-on clock does not fit that task. He questions the premium but agrees to write an ad the manufacturer likes and test it.
- On p. 157 he reports manufacturer approval, poor market response, and eventual disposal of the product at $39.95. The ad text, unit sales, and controlled data are absent.

## How the writing works

Start by translating an engineering change into a change in work. Adding a clock costs money, but what can buyers now do with less effort, sooner, or more conveniently? Sugarman's objection rests on his understanding of on-demand business use. Without a connection to the primary task, polished wording struggles to justify an extra $30. Establish the value before choosing the headline.

This does not prove clocks are useless or that the ad was good: without its text, manufacturer approval is not an independent quality assessment. Nor can price, functionality, channel, and period be separated. Have product and writing teams specify the use moment, benefit, alternative, and price rationale. Weak answers suggest testing demand or adjusting the offer rather than adding revolutionary adjectives. The later $39.95 price is a disposition price, not a documented successful version.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. For each feature, record the use moment, task, benefit, and existing alternative.
2. Compare the premium with supported value in actual use.
3. Lead with the benefit closest to the main task and explain its mechanism.
4. Separate approval of copy from market outcomes; review offer, price, channel, and wording when results are poor.

## Draft checks

- Is the point manufacturing cost or buyer value?
- What real behavior does the feature change?
- Can the test distinguish lack of need, price resistance, misunderstanding, and insufficient reach?

## Suitable tasks

- New products, upgrades, or price increases, especially when technical enthusiasm precedes demand evidence.

## Transfer limits

- 1973 prices and habits do not describe today's market.
- Do not call $39.95 a proven improvement or isolate the clock as the cause of failure.

## Reported results and evidence

Manufacturer approval, poor performance, and the final price are author reports. No underlying orders, sample, or comparison separates feature and price effects.

Evidence status: Results are reported or relayed by the source; not independently verified.

## English writing craft

**Original drafting test:** “It now has [feature]” → “When [task], you can [supported action] without [verified extra step].”

The second structure forces a use moment and an observable consequence. If those blanks cannot be supported, keep researching the proposition instead of decorating *new* with superlatives.
