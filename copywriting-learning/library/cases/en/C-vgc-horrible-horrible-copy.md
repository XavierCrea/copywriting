# Examine what a promise asks readers to believe | Horrible, horrible copy

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-vgc-horrible-horrible-copy.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

## Source observations
The author moves from childhood to a relative’s terminal illness and then bereavement before presenting an advertisement. Its image claims a cancer solution, a 97% success rate, and an all-natural treatment without displaying supporting evidence.

## Interpretation and application
The emotional sequence makes the stakes of an exaggerated promise tangible. Use this check when a headline sells hope, a success rate, or a certain outcome.

1. State the concrete result a reader might infer.
2. Find evidence and applicable conditions for each promise.
3. Put qualifications beside the claim and offer a deliverable next step.

Check that implied results do not exceed demonstrated capability and that the evidence concerns the same people and conditions.

## Limits and results
The author reports disgust and tearing down the ad. The pictured wording is inspectable; medical efficacy and campaign performance are unverified. One person’s response does not establish audience-wide effects.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original practice:** “Compare the course outline with the skills your next role requires.” Supply an actual outline.

“Compare” offers a useful action within the seller’s control. “Your next role” makes relevance personal without promising a job. The sentence replaces an unearned outcome claim with a decision the reader can make using available evidence.
