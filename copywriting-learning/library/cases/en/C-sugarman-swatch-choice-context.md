# Swiss Army watch: focus the first ad, then support comparison in the catalog

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-sugarman-swatch-choice-context.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** a Swiss Army watch; the book and ad do not support an older attribution to the Swatch brand.
- **Reader:** first-contact *Wall Street Journal* readers versus interested buyers receiving a later catalog.
- **Task:** make the initial proposition easy to grasp, then provide model choice.
- **Medium:** newspaper split editions in the same time and area, followed by a catalog.

## What the material shows

- Page 166 describes men's, women's, and children's watches in black, red, and tan: nine options. On p. 167 the author proposes only the black men's model.
- He reports editions distributed at the same time and in the same area with broadly similar wording and layout. The nine-option version adds men's/children's size comparison and a different options list; the p. 168 thumbnails also show image and layout differences.
- He reports three times as many units sold by the single-model ad. Page 263 says other models performed better in the catalog. The ad says “Join the Swiss Army”; p. 263 identifies a Swiss Army watch.

## How the writing works

The lesson separates decisions rather than declaring fewer options always better. A new reader first decides whether this type of watch is desirable. Simultaneous gender, size, and color comparisons can complicate that initial proposition. A single subject aligns image, explanation, and action. Later, interested catalog readers benefit from choice that helps find the right fit.

The two accounts are compatible: familiarity, medium, and decision stage change the value of options. Separate consideration from selection while preserving access to essential specifications. The 3:1 result is the author's account, and images, lists, and composition also changed. It does not isolate option count or justify promising a threefold improvement from deleting products on a modern page.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Determine whether readers are discovering the product or selecting a model.
2. Give first-contact readers a representative main offer aligned across headline, image, and action.
3. Put broader comparison at an appropriate later level, preserving essential fit information.
4. Record all simultaneous test changes and examine orders, profit, and audience differences, not clicks alone.

## Draft checks

- Does choice help the present decision or require comparison before interest?
- Has focus removed necessary fit, price, or audience information?
- Did imagery, price, traffic, or structure also change?

## Suitable tasks

- First contact with a multi-variant product sharing one core benefit and a natural later comparison route.

## Transfer limits

- Presentation changes prevent isolating the effect of option count.
- The historical 3:1 is context-specific; the catalog is not a repeat of the newspaper experiment.

## Reported results and evidence

The author reports the 3:1 newspaper unit-sales ratio and better sales of other styles in the catalog. No underlying orders, sample, exposure, cost, or significance data were independently checked.

Evidence status: Results are reported or relayed by the source; not independently verified.

## English writing craft

**Original navigation pair:** “Meet [model].” → “Compare sizes and finishes.”

The first verb introduces one subject; the second makes the next decision explicit. This is interface-copy practice, not source copy. Keep comparison immediately accessible when fit is a prerequisite rather than a later preference.
