# Precor: let a story’s space constraint explain why the product appears

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-sugarman-precor-fiction-boundary.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** Precor precision rowing machines.
- **Reader:** print readers potentially interested in home fitness but not actively seeking a rower; the source gives no more specific audience.
- **Task:** earn continued reading, then introduce storage and exercise needs.
- **Medium:** narrative excerpts in the author's retrospective.

## What the material shows

- Drawing on a news account of an accountant embezzling money, the author writes a prisoner story with a last-wish headline. A wife seeks easily stored equipment providing substantial exercise for her imprisoned husband.
- On p. 59, cell-space and exercise needs lead to Precor. The book summarizes the middle product section rather than reproducing it fully.
- Pages 59–60 disclose that the embezzlement story is said to be real, but names, the wife's JS&A order, and the rowing-machine choice are invented. Laundry-ball and computer openings on pp. 60–61 are separate hypothetical examples.

## How the writing works

The relevant bridge is adequate exercise in limited space, not the unusual identity of a prisoner. A predicament and unfinished wish create momentum; space, storage, and exercise scope establish selection criteria before the product arrives. A story earns its product appearance when it establishes a task the product can actually address. Suspense without that connection may leave only story memory.

Fraud details and the wife's concern make narrative vivid but do not prove performance. A closing fiction disclosure does not turn invented purchases into testimony. Transfer the constraint-to-criteria-to-product structure through verified experience or clearly labeled demonstration. Prove dimensions, storage, and exercise scope separately. The incomplete middle prevents claims to have reconstructed the whole ad's rhythm.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Start with a real constraint the product addresses, not sensational material.
2. Choose a verified experience or clearly staged situation that naturally creates it.
3. Establish criteria before revealing the product, then answer them with facts.
4. Check identity, relationships, purchases, and results; label dramatization and distinguish plot from proof.

## Draft checks

- Would the story remain unchanged and unrelated to selling if the product disappeared?
- Does the product solve a task already established?
- Could readers mistake dramatization for testimony?

## Suitable tasks

- A situation is needed to explain a function and its constraint directly relates to a supported advantage.

## Transfer limits

- Do not reuse the names, wife's purchase, or equipment choice as real customer evidence; this review did not independently verify the news prototype.
- Later laundry-ball and computer exercises are not Precor placement or performance evidence.

## Reported results and evidence

The pages document story construction and fictional parts, with no sales, readership, or conversion result.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original fictional scene:** “The spare room was already an office. Anything we added had to fold away.”

The first sentence establishes occupied space; *had to* turns the second into a selection criterion. A product should appear only after the reader understands that constraint. Do not publish the invented “we” as a customer account.
