# Rolls-Royce: an audible detail opens a larger argument for quality

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-rolls-royce-specific-detail.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** a premium car must explain why its quality merits the price.
- **Reader:** affluent buyers comparing experience, workmanship, and ownership demands.
- **Task:** earn attention through concrete quality evidence and supply reasons for an inquiry.

## What the material shows

- PDF p. 15 moves from a car photograph to the electric-clock sound headline, qualified at 60 mph, an engineering subheading, 13 numbered points, the historical $13,995 price, and dealer contact.
- Point one attributes the observation to a technical editor of *The Motor* and mentions three silencers. Further ad claims include seven hours of engine running before installation, a final week of adjustment and 98 tests, stethoscope inspection, five primer coats, and nine finish coats.
- Ownership details include design for owner-drivers, a length 18 inches shorter than large American cars, power assistance and automatic transmission, a three-year warranty, dealer and parts support, and a Bentley alternative.
- On PDF p. 16, Ogilvy reports three weeks of research, 670 words of body copy, placement in two newspapers and a magazine, and $25,000 expenditure. He also recounts 500 defective cars later and ending the relationship. These are author accounts.

## How the writing works

The ad first gives a reason to stop, then reasons to consider the purchase. Speed, cabin, and clock supply a familiar sensory comparison instead of asking readers to accept an adjective such as exceptionally quiet. Imagining the scene lets readers infer quality. The first body point supplies attribution and an engineering explanation so the surprise has support.

The subheading extends the sound detail into a manufacturing principle. Thirteen points support meticulousness from different angles. Stethoscope checks and repeated finishing suggest investment in work customers cannot see. Numbering also lets readers select the evidence relevant to their own decisions.

Luxury is presented through use as well as status: driving, parking, warranty, and parts address ownership burdens. Price and contact return the impression to a practical decision. The reusable chain is sensory evidence, manufacturing support, use and ownership benefits, and an actionable next step—not the clock sentence itself or an assumption that long copy always succeeds.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Gather technical, testing, use, and service facts with sources and conditions.
2. Choose an imaginable detail that represents a central benefit.
3. Express the perceptible result with its conditions as a headline candidate.
4. Add direct support, manufacturing or mechanism explanation, and ownership benefits.
5. Organize skimmable copy around purchase questions; finish with actual pricing conditions and a next step.

## Draft checks

- Without the brand, can readers identify a specific benefit?
- Is the headline experience supported by this product's facts?
- Does each detail add a reason to consider buying?
- Are use, maintenance, cost, and contact questions answered?

## Suitable tasks

- Verifiable technical, craftsmanship, or service differences justify involved comparison.
- One detail represents broader value, with evidence for the remaining buying conditions.

## Transfer limits

- Borrow the evidence-building method, not historical performance or a single highlight as a substitute for overall quality.
- Select details for the current decision; sensory approaches do not suit every product.
- Claims about the effect of length, numbering, and layout are mechanism analysis, not sales causation verified by this library.

## Reported results and evidence

The pages document structure, creative process, and historical product claims, not sales lift, a conversion comparison, or ROI. Three weeks, 670 words, $25,000, and 500 defective cars are the author's account. Mercedes sales figures do not belong to this case.

Evidence status: Results are reported or relayed by the source; not independently verified.

## English writing craft

**Original practice for a fictional bag:** “A place for your keys. Even in the dark.”

The ordinary noun *keys* makes organization concrete; the second fragment supplies a situation in which it matters. The wording is supportable only if the actual layout makes keys easy to locate. It illustrates moving from “well organized” to a perceptible use detail; it is not an Ogilvy quotation.
