# Cookie availability: distinguish low stock, recent loss, and demand from others

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-cialdini-cookie-availability.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** Cialdini relays Worchel and colleagues' chocolate-cookie experiments, varying quantity, its change, and the explanation for that change.
- **Reader:** consumer-preference study participants; the pages provide no sample size, recruitment, or demographics.
- **Task:** examine attraction and desire under scarcity, separately from tasting judgments.
- **Medium:** jars, tasting, and researchers' explanations, not an advertising placement.

## What the material shows

- On PDF p. 338, participants taste the same cookies from jars containing either ten or two. The smaller supply reportedly increases attraction, perceived price, and desire to obtain more later.
- Some receive ten initially, then two before tasting; another condition begins with two. Recently reduced supply receives more positive judgments (pp. 338–339).
- On p. 342, one explanation for the ten-to-two switch is demand from other participants; another is a mistaken jar. The socially demanded scarce cookies are reportedly most wanted.
- Pages 342–343 discuss both popularity cues and competition for a limited resource.
- Page 349 distinguishes wanting from taste: participants say they would pay more, but do not rate the cookies tastier.
- The p. 338 footnote cites Worchel et al.'s 1975 article; that external paper was not read for the prior analysis.

## How the writing works

There are three different inputs: low visible quantity, a recent reduction in availability, and a demand-based explanation. Recent loss differs from a consistently small supply. Others' demand introduces both “people want this” and “they may get it first.” A stock figure, change, and cause therefore cannot be treated as interchangeable urgency phrases.

Greater desire to possess does not establish better use experience. Product evidence must explain suitability; actual availability can separately explain timing. State what remains, what changed, why, and how it affects a plan. An allocation mistake is not proof of popularity, and an unchanged quantity cannot support a rush narrative. Possession of rarity can itself matter to collectors or identity-related purchases, but its basis and relevance must still be explained. These are applications of the distinction, not market laws established by the cookie task.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Identify whether the reader seeks use value, rarity of ownership, or both.
2. Record current quantity, recent changes, causes, and update times separately.
3. State only supported layers: quantity alone, change if documented, demand as cause only with records.
4. Explain actual delivery windows, waits, or reservation options.
5. Offer a proportionate next step to confirm specifications, dates, and availability; retain product evidence and trade-offs.
6. Track intention, payment, cancellation/returns, and satisfaction separately to assess suitable purchases rather than early possession alone.

Structure: suitability → current dated availability → documented change and cause → effect on delivery/choice → next step. Omit unsupported change or demand claims.

## Draft checks

- Does the count refer to all stock, one specification, or a batch allocation?
- Is recent reduction supported by two real time points?
- Is the cause demand, error, transfer, discontinuation, or supply disruption?
- Could readers infer that low quantity proves taste, performance, or quality?
- Is willingness to pay confused with payment, or preference with purchase?
- Is the product's fit clear without scarcity language?

## Transfer limits

- Identical cookies and short preference tasks cannot predict large purchases, contracts, or complex-product conversion.
- Borrow distinctions between stock, change, and cause; do not fabricate shortage or competition.
- Lower supply need not imply popularity or quality and may deter buyers worried about fulfillment.
- Genuine timing information cannot replace suitability evidence.
- Possession and use value can coexist; scarcity buying is not automatically irrational, nor is willingness to pay proof of better experience.
- No precise effect size is supplied for current sales or pricing promises.

## Reported results and evidence

As relayed by the author, two cookies attract more desire than ten; ten changing to two exceeds a constant two; demand-based reduction exceeds a jar-error explanation. Participants express higher willingness to pay without a corresponding taste improvement.

Evidence status: Results are reported or relayed by the source; not independently verified.

## English writing craft

**Original availability pattern:** “As of [date], [quantity] of [specific item] remain in this batch. The next confirmed delivery is [date].”

*As of* dates the claim, *this batch* bounds the count, and *confirmed* distinguishes a commitment from an estimate. Add a demand explanation only if records support it; the number alone says nothing about product quality.
