# Grapefruit: make selection visible, then let a household trial test the promise

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-sugarman-grapefruit-origin-offer.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** Frank Lewis Schultz's Royal Ruby Red grapefruit and winter fruit club.
- **Reader:** remote household buyers unfamiliar with this grade and needing grounds to believe its taste and quality differ.
- **Task:** make a familiar fruit worth trying, with a low-barrier first box and a continuation offer.
- **Medium:** direct-response magazine/newspaper ad excerpts with paragraph commentary.

## What the material shows

- Pages 205–206 recount an established letter-based direct-mail business, two unsuccessful agency mass-print ads, and Schultz's own draft after a 1977 seminar. Sugarman edited the headline, subheading, and a few words.
- A first-person discovery story begins with six unusual fruits on one branch, describes size, red flesh, sweetness, and juice, then explains scarcity of the Royal grade.
- Page 208 describes checks for ripeness, natural sugar, acid/juice balance, a picking ring rejecting undersized fruit, and rejection of wind marks or “sheep-nose” defects. These are process claims in the ad.
- The pp. 209–210 first-box offer is 16–20 fruits. Readers chill and cut four for the family to try. No advance payment is required; a $9.95 bill accompanies the box, payable if kept. Unwanted uneaten fruit may be returned at the seller's postage expense.
- A monthly winter club runs December through April with payment after receipt and options to skip or cancel. The ending invokes limited supply and personal taste judgment.

## How the writing works

A familiar commodity becomes a discovery the reader can follow. Six unusual fruits, color, and taste give difference an imaginable form before price. Deliberate selection follows accidental discovery, explaining why this box deserves preference. The picking ring is particularly concrete: workers visibly reject fruit against a standard. Marks and shape add criteria, showing scarcity as the consequence of choices. Translate quality into actions, standards, and the cost of rejection rather than adding technical terms alone.

The first-person voice also supplies responsibility for inspection, picking, and returns. The author says it makes a large business feel small and personal. Borrow the accountable voice, without inventing company scale or treating a job title as proof.

The household trial lets the customer assess a sensory promise: chill, cut four, taste together. Payment and return terms reduce trial uncertainty; the seasonal club answers how to continue if satisfied. Story, selection evidence, imagined experience, and offer work together; success cannot be assigned to one line or scarcity figure.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Find a verified discovery or beginning in production or service.
2. Describe two or three actual standards: who judges, with what, and what is rejected.
3. Make the experience imaginable and design a deliverable small trial.
4. Explain first order, payment, keeping, returning, future delivery, and cancellation as one path.
5. Use a real responsible person's voice, keeping details tied to the same quality claim.

## Draft checks

- Are quality claims supported by visible actions and standards?
- Can readers imagine evaluating the difference after delivery?
- Does actual selection or supply explain scarcity?
- Are payment and subscription conditions clear before the decision?

## Suitable tasks

- A familiar product has real selection standards supporting a premium, and trial helps remote assessment.

## Transfer limits

- The ad's 4%–5% grading claim and the relayed report's 4% are distinct; neither proves current scarcity.
- Historical origin, taste, selection, health language, prices, and business figures retain their source limits.
- The first box is not an unconditional gift: keeping it triggers payment and the offer includes later monthly deliveries. Use current actual terms.

## Reported results and evidence

Sugarman relays Schultz's claim of his lowest advertising cost per customer and many orders. He cites a December 1980 *Texas Monthly* report about 1979: 26,000 tons, about 4% Royal grade, 80,000 customers, 14,000 acres, and hundreds of staff. Page 212 says the ad was eventually exhausted after years of use. The prior analysis read the book's account, not the magazine or order records; no control isolates copy, product, channel, and offer contributions.

Evidence status: Results are reported or relayed by the source; not independently verified.

## English writing craft

**Original exercise with fictional verified sorting criteria:** “Each piece passes the size gauge. Anything smaller goes into a different batch.”

*Passes*, *smaller*, and *different batch* make selection observable. Two sentences distinguish the accepted standard from the rejected alternative. This is an expression pattern, not a historical quotation or a claim about a current product.
