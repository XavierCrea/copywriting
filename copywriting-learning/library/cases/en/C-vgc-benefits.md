# Ask what a benefit makes possible | Benefits

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-vgc-benefits.md) · Writing exercise

## Source observations
The author repeatedly asks what a benefit means to the reader. A hypothetical art-print example progresses from large size through a room’s focal point and conversation topic to displaying knowledge and taste. It is a demonstration, not a deployed campaign.

## Interpretation and application
Use the chain to generate motive hypotheses. Deeper links need stronger checks; imagined social outcomes are not product promises. Use when copy stops at specifications or a generic benefit such as convenience.

1. Start with a verified feature and its direct use outcome.
2. Ask what that means to the target reader, recording each assumption.
3. Choose a supported level using interviews or behavior evidence.

Check each causal step and whether this reader actually wants the deeper result.

## Limits and results
Features do not automatically confer status. Different audiences may value different levels; every chain need not end in vanity. The diagram shows reasoning, not validated motives, purchasing behavior, or sales.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original hypothetical chain:** “A removable divider → room for a larger tool → one case for the job → less repacking before departure.” Each link needs verification.

A possible line, once supported: “Make room for the larger tool without repacking the rest.” “Without” joins an action to an avoided task. It is concise because it selects one useful link; it does not force every possible emotional or social benefit into the headline.
