# Stock shipments: check the conclusion the reader may draw

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-bly-stock-shipment-implied-claim.md) · Critical analysis and hypothetical rewrite

**Source:** Robert W. Bly, *The Copywriter’s Handbook*, third edition, Chinese translation. Chapter 4, §4.3, PDF pp. 54–55. The lessons below use the examples as checks for misleading implications.

## What Bly shows

A metal importer referred to the share of orders shipped from stock. Bly says the stock belonged to suppliers and that the importer had no warehouse of its own. In another example, a service offering speech material was compared with the price of hiring a writer for a complete speech. The price comparison left a difference in deliverables unclear. The book supplies the author's account and no audience or performance measurement for these examples.

## How to use it

1. Restate what the buyer might infer about ownership, timing, conditions, or the comparison. Keep this next to what the sentence explicitly says.
2. Check whether the evidence supports that inference. Put the date, denominator, owner, or scope beside the claim where it changes the buying decision.
3. Keep past performance separate from what can be confirmed now. Offer a way to check availability or fit when the buyer needs a current answer.

## English writing craft

**Original exercise.** Of 100 orders placed last quarter, 82 shipped from supplier stock. The seller can confirm current availability by model and quantity.

> **Ambiguous:** We ship most orders straight from stock.
>
> **Scoped:** Last quarter, 82 of 100 orders shipped from supplier stock. Send us the model and quantity to check current availability.

The first sentence lets *we* and *stock* sit close together without saying who owns the stock. In the revision, *last quarter* limits the time period, *82 of 100* sets the denominator, and *supplier stock* identifies its source. The second sentence gives the buyer a way to check the current order.

## Fit and evidence

Use this edit when a claim about stock, lead time, performance, or a comparative price is technically true but may convey too much. The numbers and wording in the exercise are fictional.
