# Shell fleet oil: make the net-savings calculation inspectable

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-shell-fleet-saving-calculation.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** higher-priced engine oil needs to explain a possible reduction in total operating cost.
- **Reader:** a fleet purchasing chain with technical review and budget approval.
- **Task:** provide a calculation customers can inspect, alter, and share.

## What the material shows

- PDF p. 158 invites readers to calculate annual fleet savings. Tests, assumptions, explanations, and photographs appear on the left; projections for four engines appear on the right.
- The chart separates fuel savings, added oil cost, and net savings, explicitly deducting the premium.
- The hypothetical fleet runs 80 million miles annually at five miles per gallon; fuel costs $0.50 per gallon. Old oil is $1.70 and new oil $1.90 per gallon. Engine types and conditions such as 55 mph are stated.
- Oil usage separates 50,000 gallons consumed from 50,000 drained at oil changes. The laboratory 6% and field-reported 12% reductions concern oil consumption only; change intervals remain the same.
- For the Caterpillar 3406 column, $120,000 fuel savings less $14,300 or $8,600 added oil cost yields $105,700 or $111,400 net savings under those assumptions.
- The caption claims top readership wherever placed and hundreds of copy requests. Adjacent commentary distinguishes inquiries, sales, and profit.

## How the writing works

The premium is an actual purchasing obstacle. Annual savings translate technical performance into a managerial concern, but inviting readers to calculate makes the argument inspectable rather than demanding belief in a large total.

The chart retains the unfavorable item: oil costs more. Showing fuel savings, subtracting added cost, and presenting the net answers that objection directly. Fleet size, prices, usage, test conditions, and engine-specific columns let buyers assess which assumptions resemble their operation. Laboratory and field figures remain separate.

The calculation helps managers assess economics; photographs and test conditions give technical reviewers questions they can pursue. The same page can travel through a buying organization. A request for information supports evaluation, not an assumption that reading equals a sale. Transfer the transparent model: identify the obstacle, include added costs, use adjustable inputs, and leave material another decision-maker can check.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. List baseline and incremental costs that could block adoption.
2. Select a quantifiable technical effect with source, sample, conditions, models, and data type.
3. Convert it to operating volume, using the customer's prices and time period.
4. Deduct additional costs and separate scenarios rather than merging them into a guaranteed value.
5. Present a readable chart with enough variables to recalculate.
6. Offer a worksheet, parameter submission, or technical review; track downstream purchasing quality.

## Draft checks

- Which cost changes, against what baseline, over what period?
- Does the technical change affect only part of usage?
- Are added costs, conditions, and limits included?
- Are laboratory findings, field reports, forecasts, and customer results distinct?
- Can a colleague reproduce the arithmetic?
- Are evaluation, purchasing, and profit after an inquiry measured separately?

## Suitable tasks

- Total cost or efficiency guides the decision and supported quantitative data exist.
- A premium product may reduce a larger expense and needs several reviewers.

## Transfer limits

- Rebuild with the current product, conditions, costs, and evidence.
- Correct arithmetic does not validate inputs or causation.
- A forecast must not become a promised outcome.

## Reported results and evidence

Figures are historical test assertions and hypothetical fleet projections. Top readership and hundreds of copy requests are author reports without underlying records. Actual customer savings and sales are not independently verified.

Evidence status: Results are reported or relayed by the source; not independently verified.

## English writing craft

**Original wording pattern:** “Fuel saved: [A]. Added oil cost: [B]. Estimated net saving: [A minus B], under [conditions].”

Parallel labels make the subtraction easy to follow. *Added* keeps the adverse cost visible; *estimated* retains the forecast's status. Use current validated inputs, not the historical prices above.
