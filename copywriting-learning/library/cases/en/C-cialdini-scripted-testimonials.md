# Street testimonials: use comparable experience to answer a specific question

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-cialdini-scripted-testimonials.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** television ads present supposedly spontaneous praise from ordinary passersby; the epilogue distinguishes reliable sales statistics from manufactured approval.
- **Reader:** shoppers with limited time who may consult people like themselves.
- **Task:** the original format gives commercial claims apparent independent endorsement.
- **Medium:** street-interview television ads and Dave Barry's satirical commentary reproduced in this edition.

## What the material shows

- PDF p. 215 distinguishes selecting genuinely favorable users from actors posing as passersby with scripted scenes and lines.
- The author says praise from apparently ordinary people receives attention; the format implies natural, unarranged sourcing.
- Figure 4-8 on p. 216 reproduces Dave Barry's satire of Bufferin interviews in which everyone seems eager to ask Angela Lansbury medicine questions. It is commentary, not consumer sampling.
- Pages 364–365 recognize reliable sales statistics as useful decision information while criticizing actors creating apparent popularity for a toothpaste. This is an explanatory example, not a documented campaign study.
- Pages 216–217 and 365–366 advocate refusal to buy and complaints about fabrication, without reporting participation, losses, or actual boycott outcomes.

## How the writing works

A peer account changes perceived identity, distance, and independence. A seller has an evident motive; someone in a similar situation can seem to supply proxy experience. Street surroundings, casual tone, and apparent chance reinforce independence. Hiding casting, scripts, and selection under that appearance misrepresents the source.

Real people alone do not establish representativeness: selected praise can be truthful without describing typical users. Reliable experience under comparable conditions can nonetheless reduce decision effort. Organize it as useful evidence: the relevant task and constraints, earlier concern, actual use, change, and limits. Place it next to the question it answers. Conversational wording is valuable when it preserves observation and trade-offs, not when it conceals the brand's role.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Identify a key concern and find a verifiable customer who encountered it.
2. Ask about the prior situation, alternatives, use, and results without supplying the desired answer.
3. Select self-contained excerpts preserving conditions; obtain confirmation of edits without changing meaning.
4. Place background, concern, use facts, result, and limits by the supported claim. Give statistics their own sample, period, scope, and measure.
5. Distinguish real quotation, paid endorsement, and demonstration; retain permissions and original wording.
6. Check whether a particular concern is resolved, then examine inquiry quality and sales separately from views or likes.

Case structure: relevant role/use → specific concern → use or comparison → checkable result → conditions. Keep unreported results unknown.

## Draft checks

- Did the ordinary user actually use the product, and is the commercial relationship accurate?
- Is similarity relevant to the buying task?
- Were material qualifications removed from the quote?
- Has one person's result become a universal claim?
- Do popularity and satisfaction statistics have separate support?
- Is dramatization clearly identifiable?

## Transfer limits

- A genuine but dissimilar experience may be irrelevant; explain differences.
- Measurable technical or health claims still need evidence; popularity does not establish superior quality.
- Selected real praise remains an individual case without population data.
- Clearly labeled actors can demonstrate a situation; the concern is impersonating independent users.
- Calls for a boycott are not measured negative feedback or verified market results.

## Reported results and evidence

The author uses ad observations, satire, and a toothpaste example to explain social proof and distortion. No checkable conversion, incremental sales, or boycott outcome is supplied.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original interview prompts:** “What were you unsure about before trying it?” “What happened when you used it?” “Where would it not fit?”

These ask for experience without writing praise into the question. In the resulting English quote, keep specific nouns and conditions; do not polish away hesitation that changes the claim.
