# Fireball: move from excitement to purchase justification and ownership concerns

[English case index](../../README.en.md) · [简体中文](../C-fireball-question-sequence.md) · Source case and author/publisher account

- **Context:** Bally Fireball electronic pinball machine.
- **Reader:** household buyers attracted to new entertainment but needing to justify a nonessential $650 purchase; also company buyers.
- **Task:** turn interest into a defensible purchase and resolve ownership concerns.
- **Medium:** a direct-response print-ad opening, a flowchart, and the author's commentary.

## What the material shows

- PDF pp. 101–103 contain the opening, paragraph explanations, and flowchart, not the complete ad. The opening asks readers to imagine competing against a computer, then explains its difference from electromechanical scoring.
- The p. 102 sequence is excitement, uniqueness, why it differs, how to play, features, purchase justification, lasting play value, after-sales service, and ordering.
- The author compares the expense with televisions, pool tables, and stereos; introduces family gatherings and employee breaks; then addresses boredom, disuse, breakdowns, and repair.

## How the writing works

The persuasion changes with the reader's stage. At first, participation in a game against a computer creates a reason to read. Once interested, the reader has a reason to learn how computerization changes play. Features answer an emerging question rather than standing alone as specifications.

Liking the machine does not make $650 easy to justify. Family gatherings, repeated entertainment, and staff breaks give the desire an explainable use, while familiar purchases provide a cost reference. A worthwhile purchase must also be worth owning: boredom and maintenance anticipate regret after payment. The flowchart is useful for editing—check whether each paragraph answers a question raised by the previous one, with evidence at the right point. The transferable progression is interest, value, and ownership risk; its nine boxes are not a universal template. Lasting play value remains an advertising claim requiring product or usage evidence.

## Writing steps to borrow

1. Collect questions about attraction, payment, self-justification, and possible regret from real presale conversations.
2. Label each paragraph with the question it answers and check the transition into it.
3. Explain price through supported frequency of use, benefits to several users, or alternative expenditure rather than repeating that it is cheap.
4. Address likely disuse and maintenance concerns before the order request.

## Draft checks

- Does the order follow the reader's questions or the product manual?
- Does each buying reason have a real scenario or fact behind it?
- Can the promised answers to ownership concerns actually be delivered?

## Suitable tasks

- Durable goods, equipment, or nonessential services where interest and willingness to pay are far apart.

## Transfer limits

- Historical prices, tax deductions, service arrangements, and entertainment claims do not transfer to a current product.
- Reorder questions around the present buyer's task; nine stages are not universally optimal.

## Reported results and evidence

This passage provides no sales, conversion, controlled-placement, or replay-value data. The diagram supports study of structure.

Evidence status: Effectiveness unknown.

## English writing craft

**Original practice, not Fireball copy:** “Who will use it? How often? And who handles repairs?”

The first two short questions establish use value; the third changes the time horizon to ownership. This is a planning sequence, not three mandatory questions to paste into an ad. Give each an evidence-backed answer when it matters to the reader.
